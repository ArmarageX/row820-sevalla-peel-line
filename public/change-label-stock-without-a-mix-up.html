Close the Old Batch Before Loading the New Stock

From The Peel Line · a practical reading series

Nora Bellamy is The Peel Line's fictional editor, not a tested-product reviewer. The situations below are hypothetical examples of label control.

A stock change has two sides: the material entering the printer and the printed labels already outside it. The second side is easy to overlook. A roll may be loaded perfectly while a leftover address label waits under the keyboard, ready to reach the wrong carton. A dependable changeover therefore starts before the spindle is lifted and ends after the first new label has been matched to its order.

Treat every change as a boundary between named jobs. That includes switching from one roll to another of the same size when an old queue or loose output remains. The risk is not confined to dramatic changes in color or width. Two visually identical shipping labels can belong to different services, destinations or cartons.

The sequence here fits a small packing station with either rolls or fanfold media. Printer access, thermal-stock calibration and profile changes must follow the actual model and shipping software instructions. Nothing in this procedure authorizes reaching into a moving mechanism or cancelling a purchased shipment without checking its system status. For the broader bench arrangement, use The Peel Line's station overview; this guide concerns the moment one supply and job give way to the next.

Establish a real end to the previous batch

Choose a stopping point that leaves no ambiguity about what has shipped, what is packed and what is awaiting a label. Finish and close the current batch in the normal order system, or formally pause it with the remaining work identified. Do not leave half-applied labels or an open carton between two unlabeled queues and call that a clean boundary.

Check whether the printer still has queued work. A silent device is not proof of an empty queue: a paused application or interrupted connection may deliver output later. Reconcile pending jobs through the printer and software instructions. If a job is cancelled, confirm what cancellation actually does before restarting. A screen command may stop future output without invalidating a shipping label already created.

Account for the printed labels from the old job. Applied labels belong with their verified cartons; unused valid output needs secure, clearly identified handling. Rejected or superseded output should follow the business's shipping and privacy procedure. Never leave it in a tray marked simply "labels." A person arriving later should not need to infer whether a loose label is current, cancelled or awaiting application.

Name the new media and the profile together

Read the packaging or supply record, not just the outline of the first label. Identify the stock type, label width and length, roll or fanfold format, and the media detection method supported by the printer. Direct thermal stock and stock intended for thermal transfer are not interchangeable merely because they share a size. Thermal transfer also requires the specified ribbon arrangement.

Then identify the destination printer and intended print profile. The profile may control page dimensions, orientation, scaling, media sensing and other model-dependent options. An order application, operating-system driver and printer can each hold settings; determine which layer governs the job. Do not change label width while blindly retaining the old settings, and do not assume that choosing a familiar preset verifies every layer.

A useful pairing record is "this identified supply, through this printer, with this named profile." Include a stock code or other supplier identifier where available. For supplies whose dimensions are uncertain, use the media measurement guide before loading. A handwritten description such as "the white roll" cannot distinguish two otherwise similar materials with different printer requirements.

Clear the old output before opening the new supply

Give the old and new materials physically separate places during changeover. Return unused old stock to identified storage rather than placing it next to the fresh roll. Isolate old printed labels from the carton application area. Avoid balancing them on the printer lid or using the new stack as a temporary shelf; both positions let an interruption carry old output back into active work.

The distinction should survive another person approaching the bench. A clearly marked holding tray can separate unresolved output from released labels, provided it is not also used for current orders. Mark the status on the tray or private job record, not on a customer label in a way that could obscure information. Keep access appropriate to the customer data printed there.

Consider a hypothetical switch from shipping labels to smaller internal stock labels. The operator loads the smaller roll while several shipping labels remain beside open cartons. The printer change alone may be successful, yet the bench still has two live activities. Clearing the shipping work and isolating its remaining output before staging the internal job removes that collision. The point is separation of status, not color coding alone.

When a roll is being replaced because it is damaged or contaminated, preserve the reason for its isolation. A roll parked without explanation may be loaded again on the next shift. Consult the contamination guide for assessment; do not quietly mix the suspect supply back into ordinary spares.

Load once, then establish the intended printer state

Follow the printer's specified loading steps and safety directions. Keep hands outside moving parts, and use the prescribed stop or power state for access. Remove only components meant for routine media loading. The external holder must support the new supply without dragging, pinching or making the printer pull around an improvised sharp corner.

Route the liner through the correct guides and sensing position. A fanfold supply should unfold along the intended route rather than climb over a carton or cable. A roll must unwind in the direction required by the printer. If changing formats alters the route, check the dispensing-path guide instead of reproducing the old arrangement by habit.

Run thermal-stock calibration only as the model's instructions require for the changed media. Some printers and media combinations use automatic sensing; others require a specific routine or sensor adjustment. There is no universal button sequence. Likewise, feeding a few labels until the position looks acceptable is not a substitute for the required procedure. Control any setup output so it cannot be mistaken for a current order label.

Confirm the intended profile at the software layer that will send the job. If the shipping system asks for a printer again, verify the selection rather than accepting a default device across the room. Save the settings through the documented method when appropriate; an unsaved screen change may disappear on the next job or session.

Release the first label against the order, not against memory

Print one controlled first label for the new job before authorizing a bulk run. Use a shipping workflow that avoids creating unintended duplicate or chargeable shipments. If the system offers a preview or test format, understand its limits: a test page can confirm placement, but it cannot validate the destination and service of an actual order.

Check the live order label against its order record. Confirm the order identity, destination, selected service and any shipment-specific information required by the business. Compare the label with the intended carton, especially when one order has multiple packages. Then inspect size, orientation, complete printed content and placement of the barcode and other machine-readable areas.

Where barcode checking is available, use the approved process. A successful scan shows that some data can be read; it does not by itself establish that the data belongs to the right order. Visible text and the order association remain part of release. Conversely, a label with the correct address but clipped barcode content is not acceptable just because a person understands it.

Hold the remaining run until the first label meets both checks: correct shipment and acceptable output. If it fails, isolate it, correct the relevant setting and produce a new controlled check. Do not place failed examples in the ordinary dispenser. Only after approval should the first valid label join its matched carton and the rest of the job proceed.

Make interruption a visible state

A changeover rarely announces when it will be interrupted. A phone call can arrive after the media is loaded but before the profile is checked. A replacement operator may see a fresh roll and assume the first-label gate has already passed. Avoid relying on a person's memory to distinguish "loaded" from "released."

Leave a private status note at the station: old batch closed or paused, new stock identified, loading complete, calibration status and first-label approval outstanding or complete. A short checklist with separate marks is more useful than a single "ready" tick. The person resuming should perform the remaining checks rather than restart everything automatically or assume everything was finished.

If it is unclear which labels were printed before the interruption, stop their application and reconcile them with the order system. Reprinting blindly can create duplicates, while discarding valid output without checking status can leave a shipment incomplete. Protect customer information during that reconciliation. Ask the responsible shipping operator to resolve system uncertainty instead of improvising a physical fix.

Resolve exceptions without contaminating the next job

An unexpected label from the old queue is a reason to pause the new run immediately. Keep that output out of the current carton area and check what remains pending. Correct the queue or application issue at its source. Moving the dispenser, changing rolls again or tearing off the unexpected label does not establish that the old job is gone.

If the new stock will not register correctly, distinguish media identification from physical loading and printer configuration. Reconfirm the stock, detection mode and documented calibration steps. If the new width cannot be supported by the holder or printer, stop rather than forcing guides tighter or accepting clipped output. Use the troubleshooting guide to locate the mismatch before deciding that replacement hardware is necessary.

A supply shortage is also an exception, not permission for an undocumented substitute. Another size or stock type may need a different profile, holder position or printer configuration. Verify compatibility before using it, and apply the same first-label gate. Urgency changes the importance of a clear boundary; it does not remove that boundary.

Leave a changeover card, not a mystery roll

A compact handover record can keep the process repeatable without collecting unnecessary customer details. Record these items alongside the private job information:

  • Previous batch status and the disposition of remaining printed labels.
  • New supply identity, dimensions, format and detection method.
  • Destination printer and the profile actually selected.
  • Required loading or calibration checks completed, including any unresolved exception.
  • First-label approval against the order and carton before the bulk run.

The record should distinguish facts from pending steps. "Profile selected" is not "first label approved," and "old roll removed" is not "old output isolated." Retain records according to the business's needs rather than inventing a universal storage period. When the next operator can identify the supply, the job and its release state without guessing, the changeover has done its work.
